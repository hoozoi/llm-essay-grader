# Holistic Scoring Guide (1–6): Explanatory essay on the advantages of limiting car usage

Give ONE holistic score from 1 to 6. Judge mainly **framing (thesis/introduction/conclusion), organization around advantages, breadth and density of accurate source detail that is tied to advantages, and whether benefits are stated and explained**. Spelling and grammar matter little. Use the whole scale and do NOT default to 3. In a typical set about 7% are 1, 21% are 2, 35% are 3, 27% are 4, 9–10% are 5, and a 6 is very rare. If more than about 40% of your scores are 3, re-check: the weakest 3s are probably 2s (or 1s), and the strongest 3s are 4s. Look at your 4s too: the best ones (advantage paragraphs, several sources cited, benefits explained) should be 5s. If you give almost no 5s, you are too strict at the top.

## Step-by-step procedure
1. **Copying/fact-list check.** Is the body mostly copied, near-copied, garbled, or a list of source facts with no thesis, no introduction or conclusion, and no real explanation (only throwaway lines such as "maybe this is good")? If yes, score 1.
2. **Structure.** Is there a thesis or stated purpose about the advantages of limiting cars, ideally previewing reasons? Are body paragraphs built around advantages (pollution/health, stress, traffic, cost, safety, community life, changing culture) or only around sources/events? Is there a conclusion?
3. **Development.** Does the writer connect evidence (quote, statistic, example) to why it is an advantage? One-line reactions at the end of a paragraph do NOT count. Repeated "evidence + comment" under advantage topic sentences does, even if wordy.
4. **Sources.** Count sources used with specific, accurate details (of four). Details count only when tied to an advantage claim; narrating what happened (dates, fines, who joined) does not. Explicit attribution such as "(Source 2)" or author names, spread over three or four sources, is a strong sign. Minor factual slips and misreadings cost little; do not drop an essay a full level for them unless the source content is mostly garbled.
5. **Language.** Errors lower the score only when meaning is hard to follow (at most one point).

## Score levels

**6 (very rare):** A strong 5 plus a clear sense of audience, an acknowledged counterpoint answered with reasons, or genuine synthesis across sources, sustained throughout (for example, "quote + own explanation" under every advantage plus a rebuttal of objections).

**5 (strong):** Thesis in an introduction, usually previewing 2–3 advantages. A real conclusion. Body paragraphs organized by advantage, each starting with a benefit claim and supporting it with evidence from different sources (often with parenthetical source labels), using three or four sources overall. Explanation may be brief, the conclusion may only restate the thesis, and there may be misreadings or many errors. Do NOT lower such an essay to 4 because the explanation is "thin" or "uneven", a source detail is misread, or one source is unused. A long essay organized by source can also be a 5 if every paragraph has many accurate details and clearly states the benefits. NOT a 5: an essay whose paragraphs drift into reporting trends or events without arguing a benefit, or whose paragraphs are short source summaries.

**4 (competent):** Clear thesis, introduction, body paragraphs built around distinct reasons or advantages, a conclusion, and the writer explaining reasons in their own words. Source support may be thin (one or two sources, few details), mixed with quoted source phrases, or somewhat garbled, as long as the logic is easy to follow. Also a 4: an opinion-and-reasons essay with no sources but with intro, several distinct advantages each developed in its own paragraph, and a conclusion. Uneven explanation, errors, or few sources do not push such an essay down to 3. An essay with an intro, ordering words ("first," "in addition," "in conclusion"), and the writer's own reasons (cost, safety, stress) is at least 3–4 even if it pastes in source sentences.

**3 (developing):** A recognizable position and some relevant support, with paragraphs that have topics, but explanation of why details are advantages is only occasional. Mostly paraphrased source facts with a few real comments, generic or repetitive reasoning, loose organization, or a weak or missing introduction or conclusion. Paragraphs organized by source with benefits stated only occasionally are at most 3, and 3 only if most paragraphs clearly state why the details are advantages.

**2 (limited):** Mainly reports what sources say, usually one paragraph per source or event, with benefits only in a short closing remark or one-line reactions. The thesis is vague or only says "there are many advantages"; the conclusion is generic. **This is a 2 even with an intro and conclusion, three or four sources, many specific numbers, quotes, citations, and clean writing**, when the paragraphs mainly narrate what each source reports and the advantages are not explained. Also a 2: a brief or rambling opinion with a few ideas and little support; a retelling in which the writer gives their own (even mistaken) interpretation or opinion; source fragments plus a slight own point.

**1 (minimal):** No real argument or explanation.
- Copied or near-copied source sentences, or disconnected facts listed one after another, with no thesis/intro/conclusion and no explanation (even if all four sources are touched).
- Neutral source-by-source retelling in the writer's own words with NO introduction/thesis, NO conclusion, and no explanation of advantages, or only "maybe it's good"-type remarks.
- Personal feelings or off-topic musings that never list or explain advantages of limiting cars; incoherent or nearly unreadable writing.
- If torn between 1 and 2 for a retelling with no framing and no own explanation, choose 1. If the writer offers own interpretations or opinions, choose 2.

## Decision rules
- Mostly copied or lightly reworded source sentences, or a fact list, with no thesis and no explanation: 1. A multi-paragraph shape does not rescue it. Never 3.
- Retelling by source/event with benefits mostly in the intro/conclusion or one-line comments: 2 (1 if no intro, no conclusion, garbled, or copied). Never 4 or 5, even with intro, conclusion, and three sources with several numbers each.
- Thesis + intro + conclusion + paragraphs organized by advantage with explained evidence: at least 4.
- Meets 4 and also has benefit-claim paragraphs that cite three or more sources (clearly attributed) with specific details: 5.
- Using all four sources does not by itself lift an essay above 2; details must be tied to advantages.
- No source evidence: at most 4, and only if well organized by distinct advantages with real explanation. Unorganized or opinion-only essays are 1–2 (3 at most).
- Surface errors alone never move an essay more than one point. Do not penalize quotes with citations; judge the commentary around them and how well details support advantages.

## Calibration
- **1 vs 2:** No framing, pasted or garbled content, copied sentences, rambling, only "maybe" remarks, or neutral retelling with no intro or conclusion = 1. Own-wording report with some framing or genuine own comments/opinions = 2.
- **2 vs 3:** Each paragraph tells what one source or event did, with benefits only in a short comment or the ending = 2. Paragraphs are headed by an advantage and repeatedly say why the details are advantages = 3.
- **3 vs 4:** Can you list the main reasons, each with its own paragraph, own-word explanation, intro, and conclusion? Yes = 4. Mostly restated facts, occasional comments, or loose structure = 3.
- **4 vs 5:** Thesis previewing advantages, advantage paragraphs citing sources 3+ times across the essay, and a real conclusion? Yes = 5. Clear but only one or two sources, few details, or noticeably list-like = 4. Trend reporting without arguing benefits = 3–4.
- **5 vs 6:** Only with a counterpoint, synthesis, or genuine insight sustained throughout.
