# Holistic Scoring Guide (1–6): Explanatory essay on the advantages of limiting car usage

Give ONE holistic score from 1 to 6. Judge mainly **ideas, organization around advantages, and how well evidence is developed and explained**. Spelling and grammar matter little. Use the whole scale and do NOT default to 3. In a typical set about 7% are 1, 21% are 2, 36% are 3, 27% are 4, 8–9% are 5, and a 6 is very rare. If you are giving more than about 45% of essays a 3, re-check each one: the weaker ones are probably 2s and the stronger ones 4s or 5s.

## Step-by-step procedure
1. **Copying check.** Is the body mostly copied or barely reworded source sentences, or garbled fragments? If yes, go to the 1/2 rules below.
2. **Structure.** Is there a thesis or purpose about the advantages of limiting cars, ideally previewing the reasons? Are the body paragraphs built around advantages or ideas (pollution/health, stress, traffic, cost, safety, community life, changing culture), or around sources (one paragraph per article)? Is there a conclusion?
3. **Development.** In each body paragraph, is there a claim, then evidence (a quote, statistic, or example), then the writer's own comment on why it is an advantage? Quote-plus-comment paragraphs under advantage topic sentences count as real development, even if the comments are repetitive. Pure retelling of what happened in an article does not.
4. **Sources.** Count the sources used with specific details (out of four). Three or more, used accurately and tied to the claims, is strong. Minor factual slips cost little.
5. **Language.** Only if errors make the meaning hard to follow does it lower the score (by at most one point). Many errors alone never move an organized, developed essay down.

## Score levels

**6 (very rare):** Everything in a 5, plus a clear sense of audience and purpose, an acknowledged counterpoint or objection (for example, people who think cars are necessary, answered with reasons), or a synthesis across sources. Reasoning is sustained in every paragraph. Choose 6 only when the essay is clearly beyond a very good 5.

**5 (strong):** Clear thesis (often previewing the advantages), organized by advantage, with an introduction and a real conclusion. Draws specific details from three or four sources and comments on them to show why they are advantages. Several body paragraphs are fully developed (claim, evidence, explanation), and the whole essay reads as sustained and purposeful. May contain misreadings, repetition, or many surface errors. An essay that has this combination of clear reasons, multiple sources with commentary, and sustained development is a 5, not a 3 or 4.

**4 (competent):** Clear thesis, an introduction, body paragraphs built around distinct reasons or advantages, and a conclusion. The writer explains the reasons in their own words and supports most of them with source evidence. Source use may be thin (one or two sources), or the essay may be somewhat list-like or repetitive. Development is uneven, but the logic is easy to follow. Also a 4: an essay with a clear intro, reasons, and conclusion and a personal-opinion voice that explains its points, even with weak source use or frequent errors.

**3 (developing):** A recognizable position and some relevant support. The paragraphs have topics (an advantage or a source), but explanation is limited: mostly paraphrased source facts with only occasional comments on why they matter, generic or repetitive reasoning, loose organization, or a weak or missing introduction or conclusion. This also fits opinion essays with weak, partly illogical support. A 3 is clearly better than pure retelling, but it lacks the consistent advantage-by-advantage explanation of a 4.

**2 (limited):** Mostly reports facts from the sources, typically one paragraph per source (Vauban, then Paris, then Bogota, then the U.S.), retelling what happened with no real explanation of how it shows advantages. The thesis is vague or missing and the conclusion is generic or token. This applies even when all four sources are used and the writing is fairly clean. Also a 2: an off-track, rambling, or very brief opinion with little evidence or a few pasted source fragments but some attempt at a point, or an essay that is mostly source fragments and summary with only a slight own point.

**1 (minimal):** No real argument or explanation.
- The body is mostly copied or near-copied source sentences or fragments strung together, with no organization of its own and no introduction, conclusion, or explanation of advantages.
- A source-by-source retelling with garbled, misunderstood, or incoherent content, or with the meaning lost to errors.
- Off-topic or nearly unreadable writing.

## Decision rules
- Mostly copied or lightly reworded source sentences with no explanation: at most 2. If it also lacks an introduction and conclusion and reads as pasted fragments, give 1. A multi-paragraph shape does not rescue it. Do NOT give such an essay a 3.
- Source-by-source retelling (one paragraph per article) with no advantage-focused topic sentences and no explanation: 2. Retelling with a few comments tying details to advantages: 3.
- Essay with a thesis, paragraphs organized by advantage, and explained evidence: at least 4.
- Essay that meets the 4 description and also uses three or more sources with specific details and comments, and is sustained across several developed paragraphs: 5.
- Using all four sources does not by itself raise an essay above 2 or 3. The details must be tied to advantages. Using few sources does not cap a well-reasoned essay at 3.
- An essay with no source evidence and only unsupported opinion is at most 3 (usually 2).
- Off-topic claims, unsupported statistics, or illogical analogies lower the score by about one level but do not cap an otherwise well-developed, organized essay below 3.
- Surface errors alone never move an essay by more than one point. Do not penalize quotes with citations; judge the commentary around them.

## Calibration
- **1 vs 2:** Copied or garbled fragments with no framing = 1. A readable, source-by-source report with some own wording, or a rambling opinion with an attempt at a point = 2.
- **2 vs 3:** Each paragraph only tells what a source says = 2. Paragraphs also say why it is an advantage, at least sometimes, with a recognizable overall position = 3.
- **3 vs 4:** Can you list the essay's main reasons, with each reason getting its own paragraph and explanation, an intro, and a conclusion? Yes = 4. Mostly pasted or restated facts, or loose organization = 3.
- **4 vs 5:** Are three or more sources used with specific details and commentary, with every body paragraph developed (claim, evidence, explanation) and a real conclusion? Yes = 5. Clear but brief, one-sided in sourcing, or list-like = 4.
- **5 vs 6:** Only with a counterpoint, synthesis, or genuine insight sustained throughout.
